5 Contract Clauses You Must Review Before Signing Anything

Unlimited liability, automatic renewal, exclusivity traps, one-sided IP assignment, and dispute resolution gotchas. These five clauses cause the vast majority of contract regrets — and most people skip right past them.

N
Norcanto Team
·January 5, 2025· 6 min read
Key Takeaways
  • Always check for liability caps — unlimited liability means you could owe more than the contract is worth
  • Auto-renewal clauses can lock you in for another full term with no warning
  • Exclusivity provisions can prevent you from working with competitors entirely
  • Broad IP assignment clauses can transfer ownership of work you created before the contract
  • Dispute resolution clauses determine where and how you can fight back if things go wrong

You have probably signed contracts without reading every word. Most people have. The problem is that buried in the boilerplate, there are almost always a handful of clauses that look routine but carry enormous consequences. These are the five that cause the most trouble.

1. Unlimited Liability Clauses

Limitation of liability clauses are supposed to cap how much either party can owe the other if something goes wrong. A well-negotiated contract might cap liability at the total fees paid in the prior 12 months, or at a fixed dollar amount. This is standard and reasonable.

The problem is when this cap is riddled with exceptions, or when it simply does not exist. Some contracts include carve-outs that say the cap does not apply to breaches of confidentiality, indemnification obligations, or gross negligence. These carve-outs can swallow the cap entirely.

What to look for: Search the contract for "limitation of liability," "cap," "maximum liability," and "in no event." Make sure the cap is clearly stated and that the exceptions do not undermine it. If you cannot find a cap, that is a significant red flag.

2. Automatic Renewal Clauses

Automatic renewal clauses, sometimes called "evergreen" clauses, cause contracts to renew for another full term unless one party provides written notice to cancel within a specific window before the expiry date. That window might be 30, 60, or even 90 days before the end date.

In practice, this means you need to notify the other party of cancellation months before your contract actually ends. Miss the window and you are locked in for another year, or another three years, at the original terms.

What to look for: Search for "automatic renewal," "evergreen," "unless terminated," and "notice period." When you find the renewal window, calendar that date immediately. It is almost always earlier than you expect.

3. Exclusivity and Non-Compete Provisions

Exclusivity clauses prevent you from working with competitors of the other party for the duration of the contract. Non-compete provisions extend this restriction for a period after the contract ends. Both can be extraordinarily broad.

A vendor agreement that prohibits you from engaging any "similar service provider" in a specific industry for two years after termination can effectively lock you out of a significant market. Freelancers and service providers who sign broad exclusivity clauses often discover they cannot take on related work from other clients.

What to look for: Search for "exclusivity," "exclusive," "non-compete," "non-solicitation," and "restrictive covenant." Pay particular attention to how broadly the restricted category is defined and how long the restriction lasts after the contract ends.

4. Intellectual Property Assignment

IP assignment clauses determine who owns the work product created under the contract. A broad work-for-hire clause might transfer ownership of everything you create — including work that builds on pre-existing tools, frameworks, or methods you developed independently.

The most aggressive versions say that any work "related to" the services, or created "using company resources," belongs to the other party. This can inadvertently transfer ownership of valuable pre-existing IP you had nothing to do with the contract.

What to look for: Search for "work for hire," "intellectual property," "assignment," "ownership," and "moral rights." Make sure there is a clear carve-out for pre-existing IP you bring to the engagement.

5. Dispute Resolution and Governing Law

Dispute resolution clauses determine where and how disagreements get resolved. The two main options are litigation (courts) and arbitration. Mandatory arbitration clauses waive your right to sue in court, which can be significant. Some arbitration agreements also include class action waivers, which prevent you from joining with others who have the same complaint.

Governing law and jurisdiction clauses determine which country's or state's laws apply, and where any dispute must be heard. Being required to litigate in a different jurisdiction adds cost and complexity that can make it practically impossible to pursue a valid claim.

What to look for: Search for "governing law," "jurisdiction," "dispute resolution," "arbitration," and "class action." Ensure the governing jurisdiction is practical for you to reach if a dispute arises.
