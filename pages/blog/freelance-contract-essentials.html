Freelance Contract Essentials: What Every Clause Actually Means

Scope creep, payment terms, IP ownership, kill fees, and confidentiality clauses can make or break a freelance engagement. Here is what every clause actually means in plain language.

N
Norcanto Team
·December 6, 2024·7 min read
Key Takeaways
  • The scope of work clause is your most important protection against scope creep — be specific
  • Net-30 payment terms are standard but you should always specify what happens when payment is late
  • Work-for-hire clauses transfer all IP to the client by default — negotiate a license instead if possible
  • Kill fees compensate you if the client cancels after significant work has been done
  • Confidentiality clauses protect both parties but check whether yours restricts your portfolio use

Freelance contracts are not just formalities. They are the legal framework that defines your relationship with every client — what you are doing, how much you are getting paid, who owns the work, and what happens when things do not go as planned. Understanding each clause before you sign protects you from the most common freelance disputes.

Scope of Work

The scope of work clause is the most important part of any freelance contract. It defines exactly what you are agreeing to deliver. Vague scopes are the number one cause of scope creep — when clients expect more than you planned to provide because the contract did not specify clearly enough.

A good scope of work specifies: what deliverables you will produce, what format they will be in, how many rounds of revisions are included, what is explicitly excluded from the scope, and what the acceptance criteria are. The more specific, the better. "Website design" is a vague scope. "Five responsive web page designs in Figma with up to two rounds of revisions per page, excluding development and copywriting" is a clear one.

Red flag: Any scope that uses the phrase "and other tasks as reasonably required" without a clear limit gives the client open-ended authority to expand your workload without additional payment.

Payment Terms

Payment terms specify when and how you get paid. The key elements are: the total fee, the payment schedule (upfront deposit, milestone payments, or payment on completion), the payment method, and the timeline for payment after invoicing.

Net-30 is standard in many industries, meaning payment is due 30 days after invoice. For larger projects, negotiate for a deposit — typically 25 to 50 percent upfront — before work begins. This protects you if the client delays or cancels.

Always include a late payment clause specifying what happens if payment is not received by the due date. A common provision is a 1.5 percent monthly interest charge on overdue amounts. This is both a deterrent and a remedy if you need to pursue the debt.

Intellectual Property Ownership

IP clauses determine who owns the work you create. There are two main structures: work-for-hire, where the client owns everything you create, and license, where you retain ownership but grant the client rights to use the work.

Work-for-hire is common in client contracts and is often presented as standard. But it can be problematic if you use pre-existing tools, code libraries, design elements, or creative approaches across multiple client projects. A broad work-for-hire clause could inadvertently transfer ownership of things you created independently.

If you use reusable components in your work, negotiate to retain ownership of those while assigning ownership of the project-specific deliverables. Most clients are happy with this arrangement — they want the work product, not your underlying tools.

Kill Fees

A kill fee is compensation paid to you if the client cancels the project after work has begun. Without a kill fee clause, a client can cancel at any time and leave you with nothing to show for the work you have already done.

Kill fees are typically structured as a percentage of the total project fee based on how much work has been completed. A common structure: 25 percent if cancelled before work begins, 50 percent if cancelled after the first milestone, 75 percent if cancelled after the second milestone, 100 percent if cancelled after completion.

Confidentiality

Confidentiality clauses protect the client's sensitive information. This is reasonable and standard. What to watch for is whether the clause also restricts your ability to include the work in your portfolio or mention the client as a reference.

Some clients — particularly in competitive industries or for sensitive projects — require full confidentiality that prevents you from disclosing the engagement at all. This is legitimate but should be reflected in your pricing, since it limits your ability to use the work to attract future clients.
