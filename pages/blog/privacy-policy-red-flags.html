Privacy Policy Red Flags: What to Look for Before You Agree

Most people click Accept without reading. These are the specific clauses in privacy policies that give companies far more access to your data than most users realize they are agreeing to.

N
Norcanto Team
·December 21, 2024·5 min read
Key Takeaways
  • Vague "partners" and "affiliates" language can mean your data is shared with hundreds of companies
  • Retention policies that say "as long as necessary" have no meaningful limit
  • Opt-out provisions buried in account settings are often pre-checked to allow data sharing
  • Check whether the policy covers data you generate indirectly, not just what you submit directly
  • Jurisdiction clauses determine which country's privacy laws govern your data

Privacy policies are long, dense, and written by lawyers for lawyers. Most of them are also designed to be agreed to rather than read. But buried inside the boilerplate are clauses that give companies significant latitude to collect, use, share, and retain your data in ways most users would not agree to if they understood them.

Here are the five categories of red flags to check before agreeing to any significant privacy policy.

1. Vague Third-Party Sharing Language

Almost every privacy policy includes a section on sharing data with third parties. The question is how broadly "third parties" is defined. Language like "we may share your data with our partners, affiliates, vendors, and service providers" can encompass hundreds of companies.

Look for whether the policy names specific categories of third parties or provides examples. Policies that give you no meaningful ability to understand who your data goes to are a red flag. Better policies name specific third-party categories, limit sharing to what is necessary for the service, and allow you to opt out of non-essential sharing.

2. Indefinite Data Retention

Data retention policies specify how long a company keeps your information after you stop using the service or delete your account. Phrases like "we retain your data for as long as necessary to fulfill the purposes described in this policy" have no practical limit and no guarantee your data will ever be deleted.

Look for specific time periods. A retention policy that says "we delete account data within 90 days of account closure" is meaningful. One that says "we retain data for business purposes" is not.

3. Broad "Inferred" and "Derived" Data Collection

Most people understand that a service collects data they submit directly — their name, email, and payment details. Fewer understand that many policies also cover inferred and derived data: information the company generates about you based on your behavior, preferences, and patterns.

Look for language about "inferences," "derived information," "behavioral data," and "profile data." These sections reveal how much the company is building a model of you beyond what you explicitly provided.

4. Opt-Out Architecture vs Opt-In

There is a significant difference between a service that requires your explicit consent before sharing your data for marketing (opt-in) and one that shares your data by default and requires you to navigate to account settings to stop it (opt-out). Both are legal in many jurisdictions, but opt-out architecture dramatically reduces how many users actually protect their data.

Check for: Language like "you may opt out of marketing communications" or "you can manage your sharing preferences in account settings." These signal opt-out architecture where the default is broad data sharing.

5. Governing Law and Jurisdiction

Privacy laws vary enormously by jurisdiction. The EU's GDPR provides strong data rights. Privacy laws in other jurisdictions may provide far less protection. A privacy policy that specifies the laws of a jurisdiction with weak privacy protections may mean you have fewer rights than you expect.

Look for the governing law clause near the end of the policy. If you are in a jurisdiction with strong privacy laws like the EU or California, check whether the policy extends those protections to all users or only to users in those specific regions.
